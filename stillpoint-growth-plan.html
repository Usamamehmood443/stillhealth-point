How we grow Google traffic

Google ranks health sites on three things: clear and helpful pages, proof that a qualified expert stands behind them, and other trusted sites pointing to them. We work on all three.

On the website

  • Clearer page headings. The small label above each headline becomes the main heading Google reads, with the condition name in it. Your written headlines stay exactly as they are.
  • A "Conditions" menu at the top and a Conditions column in the footer, so every condition page is one click from anywhere.
  • Deeper condition pages. Chronic Fatigue, Thyroid and Mold grow to about 1,000 to 1,200 words: symptoms, what you look at, testing, the first visit, related conditions.
  • New pages: "What to expect" (first visit and membership) and Long COVID.
  • "Reviewed by Dr. Michael Palladino, ND · Updated [date]" on every condition page and article.
  • Linking between pages: each condition page links to related conditions and to its articles.

Technical (behind the scenes)

  • Structured data on every page (who you are, credentials, FAQs), so Google and AI tools read it correctly.
  • Redirects for old addresses, a clean robots file, alt text on images, and a page speed check.
  • After each change, we ask Google to re-crawl the page in Search Console.

Outside the website (trust signals)

  • Google Business Profile: a service-area practice with the address hidden. It's free, and it's the strongest local and trust signal.
  • Directories: AANP Find a Doctor, CNDA, IFM practitioner finder, Healthgrades, The Filtery, Bastyr alumni, LinkedIn.
  • Reviews: a simple link sent after visits, asking happy patients for a Google review. A steady flow, not a burst.
  • Mentions and links: guest articles or podcast interviews in the chronic illness, mold and POTS communities.

Conversion (turning visits into requests)

  • A dedicated "Request a Consultation" page that every button leads to, explaining what happens next.
  • An optional "How did you hear about us?" question, to see which requests come from Google and which from AI tools.
  • An instant confirmation email to the person and an alert to the practice.
  • A thank-you page, so every request is counted.

How we grow visibility in AI tools

More patients now ask ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini or Google's AI Overviews questions like "who can help with mold illness and POTS over telehealth?" These tools recommend practices they can find, understand, and see confirmed in several places.

  1. Be findable in the indexes AI tools use. ChatGPT and Copilot rely on Bing; Google's AI Overviews use Google. We add the site to Bing Webmaster Tools as well as Search Console.
  2. Say the facts plainly and consistently. The same short description on the website, Google Business Profile, directories and LinkedIn: "Dr. Michael Palladino, ND, Bastyr- and UC Irvine-trained naturopathic doctor, offering whole-person telemedicine for mold illness, POTS, MCAS, chronic fatigue, thyroid, gut and autoimmune conditions."
  3. Write answers AI tools can quote. Each article and FAQ starts with a direct 2 to 3 sentence answer, then the detail, with headings phrased as questions.
  4. Get mentioned on other sites. Directories, "best practitioners" lists, patient communities and podcasts count for more here than anything on the site itself.
  5. Show expertise. Author bio, credentials, "Reviewed by" dates and a short page on how content is written.
  6. An llms.txt file. A short plain-text summary of the practice for AI crawlers.

How we measure it: each month we ask ChatGPT, Perplexity and Gemini the same 10 questions and record whether Stillpoint is mentioned. We also watch for visits from chatgpt.com and perplexity.ai, and for "AI assistant" answers on the new form question.

Articles: steady, not daily

Articles are the biggest growth opportunity and the biggest gap against competitors. We publish one in-depth article every two weeks during the 8 weeks (4 in total), then one a week from January. We don't recommend daily posting.

Why not daily

  • For health topics, Google rewards depth and real expertise over volume. Its spam policies target large amounts of thin or mass-produced content.
  • Every article should carry your review and clinical view. Daily posting would become a review burden for you, or produce content without your voice.
  • One strong article (1,200 to 2,000 words) that fully answers a patient's question brings more of the right visitors than five short ones.

How it works

  1. We pick the topic from Search Console data and patient questions, and write the draft.
  2. You add a short clinical note or correct anything (about 20 to 30 minutes).
  3. We publish it with "Reviewed by Dr. Michael Palladino, ND", link it to the right condition page, and end with an invitation to request a consultation.

Requesting a consultation

Today every button jumps to a form at the bottom of the homepage, and nothing is tracked after it is sent. The new flow:

  1. Every button says "Request a Consultation" and opens one dedicated page.
  2. That page explains what happens next: you send a request, we reply within 1 to 2 business days, then a first consultation by video.
  3. The form stays short: name and email (required), phone (optional), main concern from a list (Chronic fatigue, Mold, POTS/MCAS, Thyroid/hormones, Digestive, Autoimmune, Prevention, Not sure) with an optional message, and "How did you hear about us?" (optional).
  4. After sending, the person sees a thank-you page with what to prepare (recent labs, medication list).
  5. The person gets a confirmation email straight away, and the practice gets an alert.
  6. Every request is counted, so each month we can see which pages and sources bring real inquiries.

Later, if you want it, people could book their first consultation directly in a calendar instead of sending a request.
